A joist hanger looks simple. It's a small metal bracket that holds a deck joist to the house. But this small part carries a huge amount of weight, and a poor installation can compromise how well a deck holds up over time.

Here's what actually makes a joist hanger work, and where installations commonly go wrong.

### What a Joist Hanger Does

A joist hanger is a metal bracket that connects a deck joist to the ledger board — the beam bolted to your house. The joist sits inside the bracket. The bracket carries the weight of the joist down into the house structure.

Manufacturers make joist hangers in several weight ratings: light duty, medium duty, and heavy duty. Deck builders choose the rating based on how much weight the joist needs to carry.

### Why the Coating Matters

Simpson Strong-Tie, a major manufacturer of deck hardware, offers an enhanced protective coating on its hangers called Z-Max, identified by a "Z" at the end of the model number. This coating resists corrosion.

Corrosion resistance matters because pressure-treated lumber contains chemical preservatives that can corrode standard metal over time. A coated hanger holds up better against that chemical exposure, especially on a deck that will be exposed to moisture. Stainless steel offers even better corrosion resistance, but it costs significantly more than a coated steel hanger — so builders weigh cost against long-term durability when choosing a fastener.

### Sizing: One Hanger Can Cover More Than One Joist Size

Joist hangers are sized to match joist dimensions — for example, a hanger sized for a 2x8 joist or a 2x10 joist. But sizing isn't always a strict one-to-one match.

A hanger sized for a 2x10 joist can also be used on a 2x12 joist. That might seem like a mismatch, but it's intentional — the hanger is engineered and rated to perform correctly on both sizes, so it isn't a workaround or a compromise fit.

### The Most Common Installation Mistake

Here's the part that causes real problems: **the hanger has to sit tight against the bottom of the joist.**

A joist hanger is designed to carry a downward force. If there's a gap between the bottom of the joist and the bottom of the hanger, the hanger isn't doing its job — the joist is essentially unsupported at that point. One deck-building contractor notes seeing this repeatedly on decks torn down for failure: a joist hanger was present, but never seated tightly enough to actually carry the load.

To get a tight fit, the hanger should be fit to each individual joist — not pre-installed on the ledger board ahead of time. Joist thickness can vary by as much as half an inch between individual boards. Pre-installing hangers before the joists are on site means you're guessing at a size that may not match. Fitting each hanger to its joist, one at a time, avoids that problem and avoids the temptation to shim a gap that shouldn't be there in the first place.

Most hangers include small flanges on each side. These aren't structural — they're temporary tabs you can bend to hold the hanger in place while you position it, before you drive any fasteners.

### Fastening Options: Nails vs. Structural Screws

Once the hanger is seated tight, it needs to be fastened — and the fasteners matter as much as the hanger itself.

**Joist hanger nails.** These aren't ordinary nails. They need to be rated specifically for use in joist hangers, with a shank designed to resist the loads a hanger carries. A standard nail isn't a substitute.

**Structural screws.** An alternative to nailing through the side of the hanger into the ledger is a structural screw rated for the same connection. These screws are engineered to resist pull-out forces better than a standard screw, which matters in seismic areas where a connection needs to resist being pulled apart during ground movement. Running fasteners through the side of the hanger, through the joist, and into the ledger creates what's called a double shear connection — the fastener is resisting force from two directions, not just one, which adds holding strength.

Structural screws used for this purpose are stamped with an identifying mark and a length code. That stamp lets a building inspector confirm at a glance that the correct length screw was used — an inch-and-a-half screw through the side of a hanger, for example, may not fully reach the ledger board behind it, so the stamp is a quick way to catch an undersized fastener before it becomes a hidden problem.

One general note: nail and screw requirements for this type of connection can vary by local jurisdiction, so it's worth checking your local code before choosing a fastening method.

### A Special Case: Fastening to Concrete or Masonry

Not every ledger board is wood. If the ledger is attached to a concrete wall or foundation, a longer screw made for wood-to-wood connections won't work — it needs something shorter, sized for that masonry connection. If you're building against a concrete or masonry ledger, use fasteners rated for that specific material rather than the standard wood screw length.

### The Bottom Line

A joist hanger is only as strong as its installation. The bracket has to sit tight against the bottom of the joist, the fasteners have to be rated for the connection, and the fastener length has to actually reach the material behind it. Skipping any one of these steps is a common reason older decks fail inspection or come down entirely.
